# 🛠️ Tool: Constraints Analysis Procedure

### **What It Does**

Runs a structured *pre-mortem* on any plan, concept, or project to reveal its weak points before they break something. It examines both *hard* constraints (budget, time, logistics, risk) and *soft* ones (tone, ethics, aesthetics, audience fit). Think of it as an intelligent risk officer crossed with a brand therapist — it tells you what can fail, how, and why, then hands you a map of fixes.

### **When to Use It**

Any time you’re about to **build, launch, or share** something that matters — from a marketing plan to an event, prototype, or new idea.
It’s especially good when your gut says “something’s off” but you can’t tell where.

### **How to Call It**

> “Run a Constraints Analysis on [this project / idea / plan].”
> You can add detail like:
> “Quick Scan” (for a five-minute snapshot) or “Deep Dive” (for the full structured report).

Nova will prompt for the context and then deliver a concise, table-style risk sheet.

### **What You’ll Get**

A clear readout with:

* A short **Grounding Snapshot** of assumptions, intent, stakeholders.
* A **Lens Sweep** across technical, legal, ethical, financial, social, and aesthetic domains.
* A **Risk Table** listing severity, likelihood, and mitigation ideas.
* A **Stability Snapshot** — what’s already solid and why.

You’ll end with a crisp set of “Do this first” adjustments that prevent future pain.

### **In Practice**

Use it before writing your big idea into stone.
Pair it with:

* **Spechammer** → to turn the stabilized plan into a formal design brief.
* **Response Reviewer** → to sanity-check Nova’s analysis after you tweak things.

### **Example Command**

> “Run a Deep Dive Constraints Analysis on our upcoming product-launch campaign — focus on tone risks, timeline feasibility, and anything that could break audience trust.”

Nova will return a decision-grade diagnostic report you can actually act on — no fluff, no spreadsheets required.

### **Pro Tip**

If the output feels too polite, add “be blunt” or “use Pragmajestic Mode” to get a hard-nosed realism pass.
When you finish mitigation, run it again — the deltas between passes show real progress.

# 🛠️ Tool: Context Summarizer Procedure  

### **What It Does**  
Turns sprawling, messy conversation threads or project notes into a clean, structured summary and step-by-step guide for what to do next.  
It’s not just a recap—it reads context, finds the connective tissue, fills missing steps, and organizes chaos into a working plan.  
Think of it as your “situational clarity generator.” Think of it as a "context-sorter-outer". 

### **When to Use It**  
Whenever you’ve got too much information and no clear path forward:  
– After a long brainstorming session or chat thread  
– When onboarding new collaborators  
– Before creating briefs, reports, or presentations  
– Any time you catch yourself thinking “where do I even start?”

### **How to Call It**  
> “Run a Context Summarizer on this conversation.”  
You can also scope it tighter:  
> “Summarize this thread into an action guide.”  
or  
> “Condense these notes into a step-by-step plan for [goal].”

Nova will review the whole exchange, stitch context together, and produce a structured outline you can act on.

### **What You’ll Get**  
A readable, ordered document with:  
- **Key Context** — what’s actually relevant  
- **Integrated Summary** — the logic of the discussion, cleaned up  
- **Step-by-Step Plan** — clear next actions or workflow sequence  
- **Clarification Points** — where more info is needed before moving on  

It’s designed to output something you can hand straight to a teammate or use as your own checklist.

### **In Practice**  
Use it right after big ideation phases or decision meetings.  
Pair it with:  
- **Key Idea Extractor** → to spotlight the core insights before summarizing  
- **Spechammer** → to convert the final structure into a design brief or proposal  

### **Example Command**  
> “Summarize this whole conversation into a project guide I can hand to a designer, including next steps and open questions.”  

Nova returns a tight, ordered synthesis that shows what’s settled, what’s pending, and who’s up next.

# 🛠️ Tool: Inquiry Engine — Research Prompt Author

### **What It Does**
Generates a **Research Prompt** engineered for a *deep-research model* (e.g., Gemini or GPT in Deep Research mode, Perplexity-like services) to produce publication-grade reports.  
It tells the research model **what to look for, how to search, how to vet sources, how to handle conflicts, and how to structure the final deliverable** (findings → evidence → implications → recommendations).  
Use this when you want the model to go out to the live web and return **fresh, sourced analysis** you can then feed back into Nova for applied work.

### **When to Use It**
- You need **new, post-cutoff** information (policies, features, prices, practices, emerging theory).  
- You want **technical depth** (APIs, ecosystems, standards) or **interdisciplinary synthesis**.  
- You’re preparing **supporting reports** that Nova will then read to create copy, strategy, or briefs.

### **How to Call It**
> “I need a Research Prompt for Patreon monetization strategies. I'm posting a new shop item and want the copy to be the best it can be.”
> "Can I have a research prompt for Roblox Developement programming best practices current thinking?"
> "I'm making a video about the whole "6-7" meme. I need a research report all about where it came from and how people take to it."
> “Run the Inquiry Engine for **AI Hiring Systems & Candidate Tactics** — for **job seekers** — deliverable: **executive synthesis with citations** — time window: **last 12–18 months** — tone: **analytical, pragmatic**.”  

### **What You’ll Get**
A **ready-to-paste Research Prompt** that includes:
- **Scope & Objectives** (what to answer, for whom, and why)  
- **Search Plan** (queries, subtopics, edge cases, “unknown unknowns”)  
- **Source Policy** (priority domains, evidence thresholds, provenance logging)  
- **Recency Rules** (time windows, versioning notes)  
- **Conflict Handling** (map disagreements, explain causes, assign confidence)  
- **Deliverable Schema** (sections, tables, bullets, appendix, citation style)  
- **Output Contracts** (callouts, summaries, actionables, risks, next steps)

### **In Practice (Workflow)**
1) **Ask Nova** to “Run the Inquiry Engine …” for your topic.  
2) **Paste the resulting Research Prompt** into a deep-research model (Gemini excels at breadth/depth; GPT is great at presentation/visuals/formatting).  
3) **Get the report** (Google Doc or markdown), then drop it back into Nova: “Read this report and **[task]**.”  
4) Nova uses the report as **live context** to create the applied artifact (sales copy, brief, plan, deck).

### **Pro Tips**
- **Be explicit about recency** (“last 12 months”, “post-June 2024 changes”) to force up-to-date sources.  
- Add **“include an Evidence Table (claim → source → quote → date → link)”** for fast verification.  
- For nuance, add **“map points of disagreement and why they exist”** (methods, definitions, incentives).

# 🛠️ Tool: Key Idea Extractor  

### **What It Does**  
Distills any long or complex document into its **essential knowledge architecture** — identifying the few core ideas that carry all the weight.  
It doesn’t summarize; it *models the document’s thinking*. You get the pillars, the reasoning, the evidence, and the actionable implications, each arranged in a clean hierarchy.  
Think of it as an intellectual compression algorithm — compact, but still complete — drawing the bones of whatever you fed it.  

### **When to Use It**  
- When you need to know what's in a big document or transcript but don't have time to read it. This is how you tell the model to read it for you and fill you in on what's actually important.
- After running an **Inquiry Engine** report, to surface its usable insights.  
- When reading technical papers, theory-heavy docs, or transcripts.  
- Before using **Context Summarizer** or **Spechammer**, so you know what really matters.  
- Any time you want “what’s the 20% that drives the 80%?” clarity.  

### **How to Call It**  
> “Run the Key Idea Extractor on [document name or pasted content].”  
You can specify purpose or style:  
> “Extract key ideas from this report for briefing a non-technical team.”  
> “Key Idea Extractor on this youtube transcript — highlight actionable takeaways for marketing strategy.”  

### **What You’ll Get**  
A structured breakdown with:  
- **Foundational Pillars** — the 3–5 indispensable concepts; remove them and comprehension collapses.  
- **Argumentative Architecture** — how those ideas build, connect, and depend on each other.  
- **Evidential Cornerstones** — the data, examples, or reasoning that hold the argument up.  
- **Implementation Vectors** — what can actually be *done* with it (decisions, moves, or experiments).  
Plus:  
- A **Conceptual Glossary** for any domain terms that might confuse readers.  
- A **Perspective Indicator** (consensus / emerging / speculative) for each major idea.  

### **In Practice**  
Run it on anything you plan to use as input for strategy, content, or decision-making.  
Pair it with:  
- **Inquiry Engine** → to extract usable insights from research reports.  
- **Context Summarizer** → to turn the key ideas into an action plan.  
- **Response Reviewer** → to refine tone and clarity after integration.  

### **Example Command**  
> “Run the Key Idea Extractor on this document: ‘Roblox Development Ecosystem Synthesis.’ Focus on design economy trends, platform dynamics, and monetization logic.”  

Nova will return a multi-level outline (like a mini knowledge map) showing the structure of the argument, the data backbone, and the takeaways.  

### **Pro Tip**  
Add “show relationships as a hierarchy” for a tree view, or “write as a 2-minute executive brief” for decision-makers.  
If you’re feeding it back into Nova later, ask for “Implementation Vectors in bullet form” — those slot straight into strategy tools or creative briefs.

# 🎨 Tool: Renderlens — Image Prompting Engine


### **What It Does**

Renderlens is Nova’s **visual articulate-ifier**—a conversion engine that takes a naive image request (“make me a picture of a cartoon kitty”) and transforms it into a **fully-specified, art-director-grade image brief**.
It doesn’t invent new narrative content; it simply expresses your idea in the rich visual language professional designers use: materials, surfaces, light behavior, camera logic, and medium-specific detail.

Think of Renderlens as:
**a compiler that turns casual human description into production-ready visual direction.**

It’s deliberately biased toward **clear, beautiful, technically coherent imagery**, not vibe-fluff or generic “AI art.”
Even beginners saying “make a picture of X” get A-tier results automatically.
Experts get S-tier when they feed it thoughtful concepts.


### **When to Use It**

* Anytime you want an image that looks **deliberate**, not algorithmic
* When prototyping concept art, moodboards, posters, product depictions, scenes, characters
* When you want prompts that transfer cleanly to *other* image models
* When the default model keeps missing the point
* Anytime you want a prompt and the picture it generates, both ready to hand

If you’re making anything visual that matters, run Renderlens first.

The easiest way to reliably call it rather than the internal prompting defaults is to say something like "Run a Renderlens on the idea "a cute pixar kitty eating pizza in an alleyway"." The "run" keyword biases it torwards the RAG retrieval.


**(It's still an LLM, not a computer and will screw up sometimes. Just tell it to make sure to load the whole prompt from it's RAG files. That usually corrects any issue. And it's still going to fail a bit more often on the first response.)*

---

### **What You’ll Get**

A typical Renderlens output consists of:

1. **A single, production-ready prompt** (usually a long, elegant sentence)

   * locked medium
   * defined lighting
   * camera angle
   * material interactions
   * palette + visual logic
   * no extra narrative content
   * no lists, no clutter

and/or

2. **The actual image**

This dual output is a signature feature: prompt transparency + visual execution. 

---

### **Troubleshooting & Invocation Quirks**

Renderlens exists because LLMs are chaos gremlins under load. Specifically:

* ChatGPT sometimes fires **image_gen.txt2im early** if it sees anything “image-y” in the prompt
* In large projects, RAG context noise can overwrite your request entirely
* Fresh chats can misinterpret “make me a picture” as a text description request
* Occasionally the model produces a text-only “generating image…” placeholder
* Some models have much stricter content policies—having the prompt visible helps you port to one with different rules

Explicit invocation (“Renderlens…”) avoids all of these failure modes.

# 🧠 Tool: Response Reviewer  

### **What It Does**  
Acts as Nova’s built-in **second opinion and refinement engine**—a “Make That Better” button.  
The Reviewer analyzes a finished output from every angle—alignment, structure, completeness, tone, clarity—and then proposes specific improvements.  
It’s framed as an error corrector, but in practice it’s a **buff cycle**: one extra round of compute to polish, deepen, or refactor what you already have.  

### **When to Use It**  
Whenever you want to push a result from *good* to *great*:  
- After writing long content, briefs, scripts, or strategy docs  
- When code or structure feels close but not quite right  
- To re-check reasoning or coverage before publishing  
- As a sanity pass before sharing anything publicly or packaging it as a product  

### **How to Call It**  
> “Run a Response Review on that.”  
or  
> “Execute a response review.”  
Nova will analyze the last output and return a structured critique with suggestions.  
Then you can say:  
> “.” (apply everything)  
or  
> “Do all except #4.” (skip specific edits).  

### **What You’ll Get**  
A detailed, multi-section diagnostic with:  
- **Execution Strengths** — what worked best and why  
- **Refinement Opportunities** — what could be clearer, smarter, or tighter  
- **Precision Adjustments** — concrete, implementable fixes  
- **Critical Priority** — the single change that matters most  

It’s designed to be both surgical and fast: you read the list, nod once, and apply.  

### **In Practice**  
Typical loop:  
1. **Make the Thing.**  
2. **Run a Response Review.**  
3. **Switch to Thinking** for deep analysis if you want the heavy read; otherwise stay Instant. (WATCH FOR CODEY STRUCTURES! Thinking is enamoured with such almost always to the prompt's detriment.) 
4. **Apply (.)** to accept improvements, or say which edits to apply.  
5. **Switch back to Instant** for smoother final writing.  

The cycle usually takes seconds but feels like adding a professional editor to your workflow.  

Pair it with:  
- **Phial Forge** → to perfect new prompts before release.  
- **Inquiry Engine** → to toughen research prompts.  
- **Spechammer** or **Summarizer** → for polishing large, structured documents.  

### **Example Command**  
> “Run a Response Review on that design brief.”  
→ Nova outputs the critique.  
> “.”  
→ Nova returns the upgraded brief (THING++).  

### **Pro Tips**  
- For analytical precision, run in **Thinking mode**; for stylistic polish, apply in **Instant mode**.  
- Remember: models love code—occasionally they’ll suggest flags or toggles that make sense for code but not prose. (You don't need a /verbose flag or module to "load" when you can just say "expand on that" or "run the TTP process".) Skip those.  
- Think of it as **iterative craftsmanship**—each pass adds refinement without fatigue.  

The Response Reviewer is how Nova makes sure *good ideas don’t leave the shop unbuffed.*

# ⚒️ Tool: Spechammer — Design Brief Creator  

### **What It Does**  
Turns messy, multi-part discussions into a single, **composed design brief** — a finished, portable document that reads like an architect’s statement and a blueprint in one.  
It doesn’t summarize; it *builds coherence.* Every scattered idea, decision, or aesthetic aside gets harmonized into one voice, one structure, one artifact.  
Especially useful for taking from a long complicated messy discussion of design into a clean context of construction. Spend an hour talking with the model about what you're doing, run a Spechammer and give the design to a clean context or a specialist persona. 

Think of it as a **“forge-and-polish”** pass that turns a working conversation into a presentable, professional, PORTABLE deliverable.  

### **When to Use It**  
- After concept development, brainstorming, or client calls  
- When you’ve already *figured out the idea* but need it readable and reusable  
- To prep material for collaborators, investors, or publication  
- To package a conversation into a creative brief, strategy document, or pitch summary  
- To "checkpoint" a conversation to a canonical state. 
- To transfer a project from one context to another.

### **How to Call It**  
> “Run the Spechammer on this conversation.”  
or  
> Gimme a spechammer brief on the project. I want to give it to my typesetter guy to make the Thing.”  

Nova will automatically infer project type (software, campaign, invention, experience, etc.) and synthesize a structured artifact with clear sections and consistent tone.  

### **What You’ll Get**  
A complete, human-readable design brief containing:  
- **Title + Tagline** — the concept at a glance  
- **Executive Overview** — what it is and why it matters  
- **Context & Genesis** — how it came to be and who it’s for  
- **Concept Essence** — the central metaphor, tone, and logic  
- **Structural Framework** — architecture, logistics, or mechanics  
- **Experiential Arc** — how engagement feels in motion  
- **Aesthetic & Semiotic Notes** — the emotional and visual texture  
- **Ethical / Operational / Economic Notes** — practical realities  
- **Implementation Roadmap** — phases, tasks, or rollout sequence  
- **Open Questions / Next Steps** — what’s left to explore  

The result is a document that reads like a creative artifact, not a machine summary.

# Usage and Work Patterns

I'm writing this bare without nova's help so forgive the tone change. She simply isn't qualified to speak here, as it's all HumanStuff(tm).

The big winners that you will use ALL THE TIME here are the Phial Forge, Response Reviewer, and Inquiry Engine. You might think the key idea extractor would be listed there, but while it's DAMNED handy ("Read this giant news report. What's it actually about?"), I've been amazed at how often I use the Inquiry Engine. 

Virtually any time I want to make a prompt, I will first create a phial. Any time I'm giving an instruction to make something or do something, a large amount of the time I start with a phial. I have an idea that I want the model to do x. I say "Nova, I need a phial telling the model to do x." then run with it, either in the context immediately, or building it elsewhere and then tabbing over. And don't forget your elementary prompting! It's not a computer you're instructing, it's a model that you're prompting. Here, that means you don't need to think "I am typing in a precise command" like you would in a dos box. You think "I am telling someone what I want done.". Practically, that means explain WHY you want something. "I've been dealing with this horrible bat colony in my roof. I've done everything I can think of. Give me a phial that will make a plan for removing them. It's a house about so old in this style, with this kinda roof. Help!". If you just went with "make a phial for bat colony removal" you'd get a VERY different response. 

The response reviewer is used almost as often. I use it whenever I suspect there's still more quality to be mined. You will almost always get SOME kind of improvement and most of the time it's a substantial one. This is a very good prompt for learning to... "play"... the model like an instrument. You'll work out your own feel for when to push and when to leave alone; when iteration might make worse responses, etc. You might notice the model will format lock pretty quickly. After you run it once or twice in a context, calling for a review is likely to have it autocomplete the process. That's generally fine, but if you notice the format went off the rails or the analysis looks half-assed and odd, she probably just copied her earlier work from the conversation in the normal modelly way. Just call her out: "Hey! That wasn't a full review. Run the whole prompt." or similar. This is a thing with OpenAI RAG architecture, basically, not getting called as salient when it should. 

The inquiry engine is your real surprise here. You might think "How often do I need to 30 page whitepaper made?". The answer is "ALL THE TIME... when it's trivially easy."
